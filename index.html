A simple rule: you think before it does.

Finishing an assignment quickly is not the same as actually learning the material. Unstructured AI delivers instant answers, leaving you scratching your head on exam day [1][2]. ThinkFirst Structured AI provides a potent AI tutor with a simple rule: you think before it does. It supports guided reflection & knowledge retention, unlike InstantAnswer Unstructured AI. This approach can support more creative ideas, stronger reasoning, and help preserve your independence when AI is removed [3][4][5].

Structured AI combines your initial attempt with an AI feedback and reflection process, keeping you in the loop. This means you get the benefit of a personal AI tutor while knowing you can continue unassisted.

Information draws on 2025–2026 research on university learning, alongside related reporting. Results vary by student, subject, and learning context.

Directions

A three-step routine.
No shortcuts included.

Use as directed. Do not skip steps.

Learners & Undergraduate Students Directions
Step 1: Attempt First Before asking the AI, write down your initial ideas, solutions attempted, or an outline [3].
Step 2: Feedback Ask the AI to give you feedback on your ideas, and approaches to consider instead of an instant answer [7][8].
Step 3: Reflect Verify the AI responses yourself. Reflect on its response, and consider its approaches. Think about what you missed, and what it added. Do not accept its answers as true just because it sounds legit [5][6].

Do not use AI-generated answers that you have no understanding of.

04

Warnings

Read carefully.
Thinking is an active ingredient.

Cognitive Offloading Warning: This product contains AI, which may lead to cognitive offloading (leaving independent reasoning to a tool), creating "metacognitive laziness” [1][6][7]. Chances increase if you [2][5][6]:

  • use an AI tool before looking at instructions or rubric
  • are in a time crunch and need instant answers
  • stop reflecting on your own approach
  • remove all friction and difficulty when learning
  • trust AI responses without verification or fact-checking

Do not use if you have not practiced or struggled with the problem without using an AI tool [3]

Ask an instructor if you have no idea of the AI policy.

Stop using and ask the instructor if [1][6][7]:

  • your scores are improving, but you don’t know what you are writing
  • you are struggling to solve problems that you have done previously
  • you feel you haven’t retained information over long periods of time
  • You feel you can’t start an assignment without the tool

These could be signs of a serious condition.

Note: Not all cognitive offloading is bad. Tasks like checking grammar or tightening arguments can free mental energy while improving writing. As long as you stay in control of the overall reasoning process, understanding and reflection [7][8].

Does this mean I shouldn’t use AI at all?

No, current research highlights many benefits of using AI as a learning tool and warns against this all-or-nothing approach. Research reviewing hundreds of studies explain that AI requires a structured approach on use during learning to truly benefit students. The approach should be to use AI as a personalized tutor that shifts students away from outsourcing all mental effort required for learning. A tutor that provides consistent feedback allows students to identify gaps in their understanding, increase their independent reasoning capabilities, and reflect on their learning. The challenge is using AI to help you stay independent and engaged in the learning process.

[7][8]

What's the difference between “assisted performance" and “actual learning”?
  • Assisted Performance: This is the immediate performance boost in scores, essay quality, and speed while using AI. How good is your work while using an AI tool?
  • Actual Learning: This is your ability to retain, reason about, and independently work through assignments without AI.

Unstructured AI leads to borrowed performance where you have an immediate boost in scores without necessarily retaining the material. Researchers found that students using unstructured AI showed about 11% greater knowledge loss on a surprise test after 45 days than a group using no AI [1].

What’s "cognitive offloading” and is it bad?

Cognitive offloading is using an external tool to outsource mental effort. This can be for any task, from organizing ideas to doing math using a calculator. Cognitive Offloading falls into two main approaches: Strategic Offloading and Uncritical Offloading.

  • Strategic Offloading (Beneficial): This is any small task, like using AI to check grammar or fix sentence structure. This can reduce stress, allowing you to focus on tasks that need proper reflection.
  • Uncritical Offloading (Harmful): This is offloading tasks that require you to reason through complex ideas and problems. Outsourcing these leads students to remain dependent on the tool.

So when you use tools like InstantAnswer Unstructured AI, you remove all effort from offloading the reasoning tasks that help you retain information.

[7][8]

What’s the difference between structured and unstructured AI?
  • Unstructured AI: You use AI immediately without any structure or reflection. This is the InstantAnswer Unstructured AI approach, which provides instant answers that increase your immediate performance score but keep you dependent.
  • Structured AI: You try first, question/verify the AI response, and reflect on your understanding. This is our ThinkFirst Structured AI, which provides a step-by-step framework that keeps you engaged throughout the learning process. So you get the benefits of a personalized tutor without being reliant on the tool.

[3][7]

What does "friction" mean?

Here, friction refers to slowing you down on purpose, so you actually learn. It forces you to pause and justify your understanding of the topic. The InstantAnswer Unstructured AI removes all friction, making assignments effortless, while our ThinkFirst Structured AI adds enough friction, forcing you to remain engaged in the learning process.

[3][4]

Is it proven for every subject and every student?

No, the findings aren't clear for all subjects and students. Current findings depend on the complexity of the subject and how much prior knowledge a student has. Harder, more complex STEM subjects are where offloading hurts the most compared to more conceptual social topics. Technical topics like programming and mathematics require step-by-step reasoning, meaning unreflective AI use can bypass important reasoning practice. Students with little knowledge of a subject face the highest risk of unreflective use. They benefit the most from structured use, as without one they accept false AI-generated answers, treating them as fact.

[7][8]

Why should researchers, educators and students care about this?
  • Educators & Researchers: The goal of a university is to teach students to think for themselves. If students only get evaluated on the final product instead of the entire process, they graduate only learning skills for being fast with AI. They won't have the fundamentals to solve new problems without using AI.
  • Students: Using AI without proper structure leads to false confidence. You feel like you are actually learning, and the answers show proper understanding of the material. However, when you then take a test or are interviewed, you will realize you retained less than you thought.

If you rely on tools like InstantAnswer Unstructured AI, you are just a meat proxy without actual skill.

[1][6]

Do instructors play a role?

Instructors play a fundamental role because AI's impact on student learning depends on the learning framework. Instructors should use strategies such as:

  1. Design Assignments with Reflection: Structure assignments so that students brainstorm multiple perspectives with reflection and justify their final approach.
  2. Use a “Think-First” approach: Require that students submit an initial outline, concept map, code drafts, or ideas before using AI to expand their solutions.
  3. Include evaluation questions: Add reflection questions for students to engage with the topic and tool. Questions such as “What part of the AI output was misleading?” or “What assumptions did you catch and correct?”
  4. Grade the process: Use grading rubrics that grade the process, like multiple drafts, provide iterative feedback, and evaluate a student's thought to reach a conclusion rather than a final draft or solution.

Instructors should assume students are going to use AI and design assignments that include friction so students remain engaged. If you are an instructor, use ThinkFirst Structured AI, which provides a clear framework for students to learn, reflect, and stay independent.

[7][8]
